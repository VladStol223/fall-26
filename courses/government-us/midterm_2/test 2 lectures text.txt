The Speaker of the House
The most important leadership position in the House is that of
the speaker, who is elected by the entire body.
• The speaker is the presiding officer and head of the House,
the partisan leader of the majority party in the House
• Constitution doesn’t say if the Speaker must be an elected
member of the House.

• The speaker serves until his or her party loses or until he or
she is voted out of position or steps down.

and can create select committees to fulfill his or her
purposes and then disband them.
Types of congressional committees
• Standing committees are permanent committees. They are
the first call for proposed bills.
https://www.congress.gov/committees
• Conference committees are used to reconcile different bills
passed in both the House and the Senate. They are appointed
on an ad hoc basis.
• Ad hoc committees, also called special or select committees,
are temporary committees set up to address specific topics.
These types of committees often conduct special
investigations.

party.

Political Parties and What They Do
Political parties are groups of people with similar interests who work
together to create and implement policies. They do this by gaining
control over the government by winning elections. Their purpose is (but
is not limited to):
• drafting legislation
• guiding proposed laws through Congress and informing party
members how they should vote on important issues
• nominating candidates to run for state government, Congress, and
the presidency
• coordinating political campaigns and mobilizing voters
Evolution of the U.S. Political Parties
• Politics during the nation’s founding was at a local interest level as
opposed to a national interest level.
• The Revolutionary War led to the formation of the two original
political parties: the Federalists, who preferred the idea of a stronger,
more centralized republic; and the Anti-Federalists, who preferred a
more confederate system built on state equality and autonomy.

Party Alignment and Realignment
• Political parties exist for the purpose of winning elections in order to
influence public policy. This requires them to build coalitions across a
wide range of voters who share similar preferences and who might
align themselves with a particular party if their preferences coincide
with those of the party.
• Depending on various factors, such as a change in the thought
process of voters or an evolution of the political parties, if the voters’
preferences are not met, a party realignment might take place.
• Todays Republican Party Realignment – example: MAGA Movement,
White – Blue Collar/Union, Men of Color (Hispanic 45% to 54%, Black
12% to 20%), Young Adults (35% to 42% note: attributed to young
men)

Implications of Partisan Polarization
• With the exception of providing voters with more distinct choices,
positives of polarization are hard to find.
• Instead of reducing interparty conflict, polarization seems to have
amplified it.
• Fewer members of Congress have mixed voting records; instead,
they vote far more consistently on issues and are far more likely to
side with their party leadership.
• A growing number of moderate voters are not participating in party
politics. They are either becoming independents or participating only
in the general election and therefore not helping select party
candidates in primaries.
