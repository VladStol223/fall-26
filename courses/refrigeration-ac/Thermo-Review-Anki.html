1 · The Vapor Compression Cycle ▼

The purpose of a refrigeration cycle is to transfer heat from a region of low temperature to a region of higher temperature. The dominant method in HVAC is the single-stage vapor compression cycle (VCC).

The cycle has four processes and four key state points:

  • Point 1 → 2 (Compressor): Low-pressure refrigerant gas is compressed to high pressure and temperature. Ideally isentropic (s₁ = s₂).
  • Point 2 → 3 (Condenser): Superheated vapor is cooled, then condensed to a saturated liquid at constant pressure (p₂ = p₃). Heat is rejected to the environment.
  • Point 3 → 4 (Expansion Device): High-pressure liquid drops to low pressure at constant enthalpy (h₃ = h₄). The refrigerant partially flashes to vapor.
  • Point 4 → 1 (Evaporator): Low-pressure mixture evaporates at constant pressure, absorbing heat from the space being cooled.

The cycle is also called the Carnot refrigeration cycle in its ideal form. Because heat is "pumped," the system can deliver more heat to the condenser than the work input — which is why COP can exceed 1.

2 · COP and Carnot Efficiency ▼

The Coefficient of Performance (COP) is the dimensionless ratio of desired output to work input. For the ideal (Carnot) cycle, COP is limited only by temperatures:

COP_cooling = T_evap / (T_cond − T_evap) [temps in absolute °R or K]
COP_heating = T_cond / (T_cond − T_evap)

Note: COP_heating = COP_cooling + 1 always.

For a real cycle using enthalpy values from a p-h diagram:

COP_c = (h₁ − h₄) / (h₂ − h₁) [cooling effect / compressor work]
COP_h = (h₂ − h₃) / (h₂ − h₁) [heat delivered / compressor work]

Real COP is always less than the Carnot COP due to irreversibilities (friction, pressure drops, finite heat exchangers).

In US practice, efficiency is often quoted as EER (Energy Efficiency Ratio) in Btu/W·h:

COP = EER / 3.412

Chiller performance is expressed as kW/ton (lower = better):

COP = 3.52 / (kW/ton)
3 · Thermodynamic Properties & the p-h Diagram ▼

Key properties used throughout refrigeration analysis:

  • Enthalpy (h): Total heat content = internal energy + pressure × volume (h = u + pv). Units: Btu/lb. Used to track energy at every point in the cycle.
  • Entropy (s): Ratio of heat absorbed to absolute temperature. Irreversible processes generate entropy. Units: Btu/lb·°F.
  • Specific volume (v): Volume per unit mass (ft³/lb). Inverse of density.
  • Saturation temperature: Temperature at which a pure fluid changes phase at a given pressure. On the high side this is the condensing temperature (t_c); on the low side, the evaporating temperature (t_e).

The pressure-enthalpy (p-h) diagram has enthalpy on the x-axis and pressure on the y-axis. The dome-shaped curve separates:

  • Left of dome: subcooled liquid
  • Under the dome: two-phase mixture
  • Right of dome: superheated vapor

A superheat of 10–20°F at compressor inlet protects against liquid slugging. Subcooling of ~15°F at the condenser outlet ensures 100% liquid enters the expansion device and increases system capacity.

4 · Real Cycle Irreversibilities ▼

The second law of thermodynamics governs real system behavior. Every real process generates entropy, reducing efficiency below the Carnot ideal. Key irreversibilities in a real VCC:

  • Superheated inlet gas → lower density → reduced mass flow → reduced capacity
  • Pressure drops across valves, ports, mufflers → more work required
  • Condenser operates at temperatures above the ideal condensing temperature
  • Evaporator operates at temperatures below the ideal evaporating temperature
  • Heat transfer through expansion devices → not truly isenthalpic
  • Zeotropic refrigerant blends exhibit temperature glide during phase change → lower efficiency

An isentropic process (reversible adiabatic) has constant entropy (s₁ = s₂). An adiabatic process has no heat exchange with surroundings. An adiabatic expansion across the expansion valve is modeled as isenthalpic (h₃ = h₄).

5 · Refrigerants: Types, Numbering & Environment ▼

The refrigerant numbering system for methane/ethane/propane derivatives:

R-[C−1][H+1][F] e.g. R-22 = CHClF₂ → R-[1−1][1+1][2] = R-022

Refrigerant families:

  • CFCs (R-1xx, R-5xx): High ozone depletion; phased out by 1996 Montreal Protocol
  • HCFCs (R-22): Lower ODP; phase-out target ~2020. ODP = 0.034
  • HFCs (R-134a, R-410a, R-407c): Zero ODP; still have GWP concerns

Environmental metrics:

  • ODP (Ozone Depletion Potential): Relative to R-11. CFCs ≤1.0, HFCs = 0.
  • GWP (Global Warming Potential): Relative to CO₂. HFCs can be very high.
  • TEWI (Total Equivalent Warming Impact): Combines GWP + energy consumption effect.

ASHRAE Standard 34 safety classification: Letter = toxicity (A = low, B = toxic); Number = flammability (1 = none, 2 = low, 3 = high). e.g., R-134a = A1.

6 · Expansion Devices & Compressor Types ▼

Expansion Devices:

  • Thermostatic Expansion Valve (TXV): Senses suction superheat via a bulb on the suction line. As load increases → superheat rises → bulb pressure rises → valve opens → more refrigerant flow. Self-regulating.
  • Capillary tube: Fixed-length, fixed-diameter tube. Sized for a specific operating point. Used in small units and refrigerators. Simple but cannot modulate.
  • Short-tube restrictor: Compact version of cap tube; can act as a check valve in reverse for heat pump applications.
  • Four-way reversing valve: Routes refrigerant flow for heat pump mode switching — swaps roles of indoor/outdoor coils.

Compressor Types:

  • Hermetic reciprocating: Motor + compressor sealed in pressure vessel. Common in small-to-medium units.
  • Hermetic scroll: Orbiting scroll wraps gas in tighter spirals. Fewer moving parts; slightly higher efficiency than reciprocating.
  • Rotary vane: Used in medium applications.
  • Twin-screw (open drive): Larger commercial systems; connected to motor via shaft.
  • Centrifugal: Large commercial chillers. Highest capacity.
