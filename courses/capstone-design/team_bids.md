Water scarcity is increasing demand for distributed systems that can produce water close to the point of use. WattAir is developing an autonomous atmospheric water harvesting device that captures moisture from ambient air using sorbent-coated heat exchangers, then applies heat to release the captured vapor for condensation and collection. A field-ready unit must operate across changing temperature and relative-humidity conditions over a full day while coordinating airflow, refrigerant flow, heating, condensation, filtration, and isolation of the internal air path. The engineering challenge is to integrate these functions into a durable system capable of producing approximately 40–80 liters of water per day without excessive pressure drop, parasitic fan power, thermal loss, or contamination of the heat-exchanger surfaces.
Concept
The primary purpose is to design and validate a semi-modular thermal and airflow architecture for an autonomous 40–80 L/day atmospheric water harvesting device. The team will model the full 24-hour operating cycle using representative weather data; develop operating logic that adjusts airflow and refrigerant flow as ambient temperature and humidity change; and design the heat-exchanger integration, automatic doors/dampers, filtration, ducting, insulation, and service
interfaces. The final design should quantify where heat is transferred into the sorbent-coated heat exchangers, where heat is lost to the enclosure and environment, and how airflow pressure drop and humidity depletion through the system affect water production.
Design Considerations
Target nominal daily water production of 40–80 L/day under defined representative design conditions, with predicted output reported across a range of hourly temperature and relative-humidity profiles.

Develop a full-day mass and energy balance covering adsorption, isolation/transition, desorption, vapor transport, condensation, water collection, and system reset.

Integrate one or more sorbent-coated heat exchangers supplied or specified by WattAir; the proprietary sorbent formulation is outside the student design scope.

Evaluate airflow distribution through the heat exchangers, including fan selection, bypass/leakage, flow uniformity, system pressure drop, humidity drop across each stage, and associated parasitic power.

Develop a control strategy that adjusts fan speed, valve position, refrigerant flow, and cycle timing in response to ambient weather, inlet/outlet humidity, temperatures, pressure drop, and system state.

Design automatic doors, dampers, or shutters that expose the system to ambient air during moisture capture and seal or redirect the air path during regeneration and condensation, with provisions for fail-safe operation and maintenance access.

Select and integrate replaceable air filtration that protects heat-exchanger surfaces over long-duration outdoor operation while limiting pressure drop and providing a practical service interval or filter-health indicator.

Quantify useful heat delivered to the active material and heat exchanger versus heat lost through the enclosure, ducting, thermal bridges, leakage, and exhaust; recommend insulation and sealing improvements.

Create a semi-modular mechanical architecture with defined electrical, refrigerant, airflow, sensor, drainage, and mounting interfaces so improved components can be retrofitted without redesigning the entire unit.

Design for outdoor operation, condensate management, corrosion resistance, safe access to moving and hot components, and reasonable fabrication using commercially available components and Georgia Tech facilities.

Provide CAD, engineering calculations, component selections, a controls/state diagram, bill of materials, fabrication package, and a validated subsystem or representative proof-of-concept test rig.

Define measurable acceptance criteria and test the most consequential design assumptions, especially airflow/pressure drop, enclosure leakage, door/damper performance, insulation losses, and sensor-based control response.
Desired Skills
Heat transfer, thermodynamics, psychrometrics, refrigeration/HVAC, and fluid mechanics.

Airflow/duct design, fan and filter selection, pressure-drop analysis, and ideally CFD or reduced-order flow modeling.

Mechanical design, CAD, modular architecture, sealing, insulation, corrosion-aware material selection, and design for fabrication/assembly.

Sensors, actuators, controls, data acquisition, embedded systems, or mechatronics for automatic doors/dampers and cycle control.

Experimental design, prototyping, instrumentation, data analysis, and clear technical communication. A multidisciplinary ME/ECE team would be well suited.

Technoeconomic/Business modelling fundamental for informed levelized cot of water of the device.

Communication skills to do rudimentary customer discovery for understanding problems and opportunities associated with water scarcity.

Vision
Our vision is a semi-modular thermal and airflow architecture built around a validated mass and energy balance, that would allow the allocation of water from ambient air to combat the increasingly growing issue of water scarcity.

Before bidding, our team contacted Dr. Joseph Mooney, for clarification on scope. His response revised two items from the posted description, and we have built our approach around it. The daily target is 20 to 30 L/day. The working fluid is heated water-glycol circulated single-phase, not a refrigerant, which changes heater sizing, loop control, and condenser strategy. He confirmed one fixed constraint: a sorbent-coated heat exchanger with an approximately 1m x 1m face and 40 mm depth. Everything downstream of it is ours to design.

The architecture would define how the heat exchangers integrate with ducting, airflow paths, automatic doors and dampers, filtration, insulation, and drainage, along with the interfaces that let parts be swapped later without a redesign. Doors and dampers would be driven by sensors and actuators on an arduino or raspberry pi. That same controller adjusts fan speed as ambient conditions change throughout the day. For the heat exchanger, we’ll need a transient model rather than a steady state one. A control strategy would adjust fan speed, damper position, glycol flow, and cycle timing as ambient temperature and humidity change through the day, using humidity, temperature, and pressure sensors as feedback. 

Success Criteria
Produce 20 to 30 L/day under the defined design conditions, per Dr. Mooney's clarification, with predicted output across a range of hourly temperature and humidity profiles, and with the architecture sized so higher capacity comes from adding heat exchanger modules rather than redesigning the unit

Move air evenly through the heat exchangers, with measured pressure drop and humidity drop at each stage, without fan power eating into net production

Seal the air path during regeneration so vapor reaches the condenser instead of escaping, with fail-safe behavior and access for maintenance

Separate the heat that reaches the sorbent from the heat lost to the enclosure, ducting, and exhaust, and recommend insulation and sealing changes based on measurements
