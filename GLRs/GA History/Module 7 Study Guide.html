1 — Hartsfield-Jackson: Origins & Scale ▼

William B. Hartsfield founded the airport in 1925 on an abandoned racetrack south of Atlanta. The site was chosen specifically because it had room to grow — Atlanta had just been designated a stop on the federal mail route from New York to Miami, and expansion was inevitable from day one. Hartsfield served as the airport's first commissioner.

In 2003, the name of former mayor Maynard Jackson was added to the airport's title. Jackson was the first African American mayor of a major southern city, and he led a major redevelopment of the airport in the 1970s.

By the numbers: the entire complex covers 4,700 acres, with a 130-acre terminal complex housing more than 200 concession outlets. In 2019, over 110 million passengers passed through — one of the highest totals in the world.

2 — Runways, Gates & Getting Around ▼

The airport has 5 parallel runways, all running east-to-west. Two are north of the terminals and three are south. Because all five run in the same direction, aircraft never have crossing flight paths, allowing near-continuous operation — in good weather, 90+ aircraft can land and 100+ can depart per hour.

Inside runways (closest to terminals) are the longest because planes are heaviest at takeoff. The longest is 12,390 feet — over two miles. The outside runways used for landing are 9,000 feet each and carry specialized Category III Instrument Landing Systems (ILS) that let modern aircraft land themselves in zero-visibility conditions using radio beams.

Once through security, passengers reach 192 gates across Concourses A–F via an underground transportation mall. The mall has moving sidewalks and 9 four-car automated trains. Putting passengers underground lets even the tallest jets taxi straight to any gate above.

3 — Air Traffic Control: 4 Layers ▼

Managing one of the world's busiest airports requires four nested layers of control:

  1. Ramp Control — Airlines manage ramp controllers who oversee aircraft at gates and on ramps. They sit in observation rooms atop the terminal.
  2. Ground & Tower Control (FAA) — FAA controllers steer aircraft between ramps and runways (ground), and manage aircraft just before landing, on the runway, and just after takeoff (tower). They work from the airport tower using eyes, binoculars, and radar.
  3. TRACON — The Terminal Radar Approach Control in Peachtree City handles aircraft within about 40 miles. Controllers use radar screens only — the planes are too far to see.
  4. ARTCC — The Air Route Traffic Control Center in Hampton (Henry County) manages en-route aircraft over most of Georgia, Alabama, and neighboring states.
4 — Expansion Milestones ▼

In 2000, Atlanta City Council approved a capital improvement program. The results unfolded over the next decade:

  • 2006 — Fifth runway completed, along with a 400-foot air traffic control tower, the tallest in North America.
  • 2012 — The Maynard H. Jackson Jr. International Terminal opened with 12 new gates, completing the 1999 Master Plan.
5 — Atlanta's Road to the 1996 Olympics ▼

Atlanta didn't just stumble into hosting the Olympics — it was a calculated, decade-long campaign.

The story starts in 1984, when Los Angeles hosted the Olympics and turned a profit using private-sector funding. This "game changer" model made cities worldwide reconsider Olympic bids as investments rather than liabilities — previous games in Mexico City (1968), Munich (1972), and Montreal (1976) had been plagued by disaster, political unrest, and financial ruin.

In 1987, Atlanta resident Billy Payne revived the Olympic idea — inspired by a successful church fundraising campaign — and pitched it to Mayor Andrew Young. Young embraced it; international investment was already his top priority.

Payne assembled the "Atlanta Nine" — lawyers, executives, and fundraisers — to run the bid. The group was hand-picked through professional networks and was affluent and all-white, which drew criticism given Atlanta's diverse population.

6 — Winning the Bid: National & International ▼

First, Atlanta had to win domestically. In 1988, the Atlanta Organizing Committee (AOC) beat Nashville, San Francisco, and Minneapolis-St. Paul in a 14-2 vote to earn USOC backing.

Then came the international campaign. The AOC toured IOC representatives in their home countries, decorated Atlanta with billboards, hosted city tours highlighting the city's civil rights history, and even commissioned a song: "The World Has One Dream."

The crown jewel of the presentation was a virtual reality video created with Georgia Tech — viewers flew through a 3D model of Atlanta. The studio PIXAR was consulted. It was groundbreaking computer-generated animation for the time.

The competition was stiff. Athens was the sentimental favorite (1996 marked the 100th anniversary of the modern Games), and Toronto was a strong rival. But Athens had an unstable economy, Toronto faced visible protesters in Tokyo, Belgrade had political unrest, Manchester lacked facilities, and Melbourne's time zone posed broadcast problems. Atlanta's strong campaign outweighed its underdog status.

On September 18, 1990, IOC president Juan Antonio Samaranch announced Atlanta as the host city in Tokyo.

7 — The Paralympic Bid: A Separate Fight ▼

Atlanta's Olympic win didn't automatically mean it would host the Paralympic Games — those bids were completely separate. ACOG (the Olympic planning committee) was initially reluctant and unprepared to take on the additional event.

Atlanta's Shepherd Center stepped up as the primary early financial and operational supporter. They submitted a successful bid to the International Paralympic Committee at the 1992 Winter Paralympic Games in Tignes, France. Atlanta ultimately hosted both the Olympic and 10th Paralympic Games in 1996 — a unity that helped push future international agencies to combine their planning processes.
