1 — Georgia's State Flag: Early History ▼

Georgia has more state flags than any other state — and the story of how that happened reveals deep tensions over the state's identity. Before the Civil War, Georgia had no official state flag. After Georgia seceded from the Union on January 19, 1861, newspaper reports described various secession flags, including the well-known Bonnie Blue Flag — a single star on a solid background.

In 1879, state senator Heman H. Perry introduced Georgia's first official state flag, based on the first national flag of the Confederacy (the "Stars and Bars"). In 1902, the General Assembly added the state coat of arms to the blue field. Over the next decades — through the 1920s — the entire state seal replaced the coat of arms, though no official record exists of who authorized the change.

In 1935, Governor Eugene Talmadge signed a pledge of allegiance to the state flag incorporating the motto "Wisdom, Justice, and Moderation."

2 — The Controversial 1956 Flag ▼

The most significant — and controversial — flag change came in 1956. Attorney John Sammons Bell campaigned to substitute the Confederate battle flag for the bars on Georgia's flag. The political climate was charged: Governor Marvin Griffin's entire 1956 session was devoted to "massive resistance" to the Supreme Court's Brown v. Board of Education decisions (1954–55), which ordered the desegregation of public schools.

The new flag gave two-thirds of Georgia's banner to the Confederate battle emblem. Representative Denmark Groover told the press the flag "will show that we in Georgia intend to uphold what we stood for." The bill passed with no public hearings.

By the late 1960s, Georgians began calling the flag divisive. In 1969, Janet Merritt introduced the first bill to return to the pre-1956 flag. By the 1980s, Atlanta city officials refused to fly the 1956 flag at municipal buildings. Governor Zell Miller tried to change it in 1993 but abandoned the fight after strong rural opposition nearly cost him reelection. Even the 1996 Olympic Games did not prompt a change.

3 — New Flags: 2001 and 2003 ▼

Governor Roy Barnes, elected in 1998 with strong Black political support, worked privately with architect Cecil Alexander on a new flag design. Alexander proposed placing the Georgia seal in the center with five small historical flags — including the Confederate battle flag — on a gold ribbon captioned "Georgia's History."

Barnes used his informal powers to rush HB 16 through the House before opposition could organize. The Senate passed it on January 30, 2001. A critical factor: Denmark Groover — then 79 and dying of cancer — admitted that anger over integration had indeed driven the 1956 flag change. Though the design was rated the worst-designed state flag in North America, its political significance was clear.

"Flaggers" organized against Barnes; he lost his reelection bid to Sonny Perdue in an upset. Perdue introduced HB 380, which passed by 90–89 (only after the Speaker cast the deciding vote). Signed on May 8, 2003, the new flag placed Georgia's coat of arms inside a circle of thirteen stars on the first national Confederate flag — Georgia's third flag in twenty-seven months. A statewide referendum on March 2, 2004 confirmed the design with over 73% of voters.

4 — Ted Turner: Media Titan ▼

Robert Edward "Ted" Turner was born in Cincinnati, Ohio, on November 19, 1938. His family moved to Savannah when he was nine. He attended Brown University, where he captained the sailing team. After his father's suicide in 1963, Ted assumed control of Turner Advertising, which became the fifth-largest billboard company in America.

In 1970, Turner bought WJRJ, a small UHF Atlanta station. By 1976 it was beamed via satellite nationwide as a "Superstation" — later renamed WTBS. He bought the Atlanta Braves (1976) and the Atlanta Hawks, keeping both franchises in the city. His boat Courageous won the 1977 America's Cup.

CNN (Cable News Network) launched on June 1, 1980 with 1.7 million homes. It became internationally respected — especially after its comprehensive coverage of the 1990–91 Persian Gulf War. Turner also launched Headline News, TNT, Cartoon Network, and Turner Classic Movies. In 1991, Turner was named Time magazine's Man of the Year and the Braves reached the World Series (winning in 1995).

5 — Turner: MGM, Goodwill Games & Philanthropy ▼

Turner's biggest misstep was buying MGM Studios for a reported $1.5 billion — considered overvalued — leaving him more than $2 billion in debt and his empire in danger.

Despite this, Turner launched the Goodwill Games in Moscow (1986) — a made-for-TV Olympic-style event. He lost an estimated $20 million but met Soviet Premier Mikhail Gorbachev and later claimed the games helped end the Cold War. The games continued through Seattle (1990), St. Petersburg (1994), New York City (1998), Lake Placid (2000), and finally Brisbane, Australia (2001).

In 1996, Turner Broadcasting merged with Time Warner, effectively ending his career as principal of a major company. In 1997, Turner pledged $1 billion to the United Nations — believed to be the largest single private donation in UN history. He also founded the Turner Foundation (environmental conservation) and the Nuclear Threat Initiative ($250 million). Turner died on May 6, 2026, at age 87.

6 — Georgia Film Industry: 1970s–1990s ▼

Deliverance (1972), shot along the Chattooga River in Rabun County, was the breakthrough film that brought Georgia to Hollywood's attention. Governor Jimmy Carter established a state film commission in 1973 to market Georgia as a shooting location.

Smokey and the Bandit (1977) and its sequel (1980) starring Burt Reynolds launched a national car-chase craze, filmed on Georgia highways and at Atlanta Motor Speedway. CBS's The Dukes of Hazzard (1979–85) filmed early episodes in and around Covington.

In the 1980s–90s, Driving Miss Daisy (1989) won Best Picture and Best Actress (Jessica Tandy). Forrest Gump (1994) became linked with Savannah via Chippewa Square. Glory (1990) filmed in Savannah and Georgia's coastal areas. Fried Green Tomatoes (1991) transformed a café in Juliette, Monroe County into a permanent tourist attraction.

7 — The 2008 Tax Credit & Hollywood of the South ▼

Georgia faced stiff competition from states and Canada offering film tax credits. After a Ray Charles biopic was produced in Louisiana (2004), Georgia passed the Georgia Entertainment Industry Investment Act (2005), boosting state film revenue from $124 million (2004) to $475 million (2006).

In May 2008, Governor Perdue signed a revised Act at Turner Broadcasting System studios: a 20% base tax credit with an additional 10% credit for featuring the Georgia peach logo. British Pinewood Studios built their first US location in Fayette County in 2013 (now called Trilith).

From 2013–2017, more projects filmed in Georgia than in all previous decades combined — earning Atlanta the nickname "Hollywood of the South." Avengers: Endgame (2019), filmed at Pinewood Atlanta, earned $2.5 billion at the box office. Georgia's film industry also includes the Atlanta Film Festival, film schools at SCAD and Georgia State, music video production, and a growing video game development sector.
